Overview

From no tagline to Live Más.

Taco Bell was founded by Glen Bell in Downey, California in 1962 and operated for fifteen years without a national advertising tagline, growing to more than a thousand locations on the strength of its menu and price point alone. That changed in 1977 when PepsiCo — which had acquired the chain the previous year — brought the marketing infrastructure required to push a coordinated national voice. The Fresh Food Place became the first tagline with genuine national reach.

What followed was a forty-year search for the right register. The 1980s moved quickly through four slogans in roughly a decade, each attempting to land on a differentiator — freshness, customisation, friendliness — before the chain discovered in 1988 that its most powerful advertising asset was atmosphere rather than product. Make a Run for the Border ran for six years and embedded itself in American vernacular, particularly among the late-night college audience that would remain a core demographic for decades.

The 1990s produced Taco Bell's most studied campaign: Yo Quiero Taco Bell and Gidget the Chihuahua. Brand awareness hit record highs while same-store sales declined — a real-world demonstration that recognition and purchase intent are distinct quantities. The campaign was retired in 2001 and the subject of a $42 million lawsuit over creative ownership.

Think Outside the Bun then ran for eleven years, the longest primary slogan in the chain's history. It reframed the taco as a direct competitive alternative to the hamburger, and found its clearest product expression in the Crunchwrap Supreme (2005) and the Fourthmeal late-night sub-brand (2006). In 2012, at the 50th anniversary mark, the chain replaced it with two words: Live Más. Launched alongside Doritos Locos Tacos — the best-selling product launch in fast-food history — the tagline repositioned Taco Bell from a food destination to a lifestyle brand. It is still active today.

"The Fresh Food Place"

Taco Bell's first nationally-pushed tagline leaned into "fresh" as a differentiator at a time when competitors were mostly frozen and microwaved. In the late '70s fast-food context, the claim was genuinely counterintuitive — which is exactly why it worked as positioning.

"Make a Run for the Border"

The first era-defining tagline. "The Border" became a brand asset — a mythologized place that turned Taco Bell from a food stop to a destination. Late-night college culture adopted it organically and it entered everyday speech. It did what Budweiser and Marlboro had figured out earlier: sell the feeling, not the product.

"Yo Quiero Taco Bell"

Gidget the chihuahua was everywhere — plush toys, late-night TV, the cultural conversation. Brand awareness hit all-time highs. Sales went down. The dog was famous. The food wasn't ordered. Business schools still teach it as a landmark case study in the gap between brand awareness and purchase conversion.

"Think Outside the Bun"

The longest-running primary slogan in Taco Bell history — 11 years. A direct competitive attack on McDonald's and Burger King: the taco as the smarter alternative to the boring burger. The Crunchwrap (2005) gave it a product to point at. The Fourthmeal sub-brand (2006–12) ran concurrently, codifying the late-night economy.

"Live Más"

Two words. Fourteen years. Launched at the 2012 NBA All-Star Saturday Night alongside Doritos Locos Tacos — the best-selling product launch in Taco Bell history. Repositioned the brand from fast food to lifestyle. It now hosts weddings, has built a hotel, and staged a musical with Doja Cat. The two words are elastic enough to hold all of it.

Legal deep dive · Wrench LLC v. Taco Bell Corp.

The lawsuit behind the campaign.

Taco Bell's most culturally dominant campaign ended not with a creative refresh but with a federal lawsuit. The Chihuahua era — which ran from 1997 to 2001 under the "Yo Quiero Taco Bell" tagline — was built on a concept that two Michigan designers, Thomas Rinks and Joseph Shields of Wrench LLC, had pitched to Taco Bell executives in 1996 under an informal understanding that they would be paid if the idea was used. That understanding was never put in writing. Eight years of litigation followed, ending in a $42 million jury verdict — one of the largest in advertising history.
