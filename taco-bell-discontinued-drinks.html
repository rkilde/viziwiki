Overview

Five decades of drinks that didn’t make it

7 categories · 1962–2025

Taco Bell’s fountain has been rebuilt from the ground up at least three times. The first version was a standard Coca-Cola setup — Coke, Diet Coke, Sprite, and the calorie-free cola TAB — installed when Glen Bell opened his early restaurants in the 1960s and maintained for over three decades. That entire lineup vanished overnight in October 1997 when PepsiCo spun out its restaurant chains as Tricon Global Restaurants (later Yum! Brands). The spinoff came with a perpetual contract: every Taco Bell fountain, worldwide, would pour Pepsi products exclusively. In a single corporate transaction, four drinks disappeared and four replacements took their places. No customer was consulted. No petition was filed. The Coke era simply ended.

The Pepsi fountain ran more or less unchanged for seven years. Then, in February 2004, a tropical-lime Mountain Dew variant called Baja Blast appeared on a single dedicated valve — the first soda PepsiCo had ever manufactured exclusively for one restaurant chain. Baja Blast proved that a proprietary drink could drive foot traffic on its own, and what followed was two decades of experimentation: PepsiCo and Taco Bell cycling exclusive flavors, premium formats, and licensed brand partnerships through the fountain, the freezer wall, and the cooler. Most of those experiments failed. This page is the record of their failures.

The most ambitious was the Frutista Freeze program, launched in May 2008 as a permanent line of premium blended beverages topped with real strawberry slices and made with natural sucrose instead of high-fructose corn syrup. Frutista was Taco Bell’s bid to compete with Starbucks-tier frozen drinks — ten flavors rotated across nine seasonal windows, each hand-built per order with fruit purée and syrup pumps. It was delicious, slow to make, expensive to staff, and operationally miserable at scale. In January 2013, every Frutista machine was pulled and replaced with automated Taylor Company granita units that could dispense a Baja Blast Freeze in four seconds. The Frutista era was over. The modern Freeze wall was born.

That same Taylor rollout killed or displaced a cascade of other products. Mountain Dew Sangrita Blast, a cherry-citrus exclusive that had held the second dedicated Dew valve since 2010, lost its slot. The Limeade Sparklers — a hand-mixed Sierra Mist and lime program designed to compete with Sonic — couldn’t survive alongside the new frozen infrastructure. Hot beverages, already marginal, were absorbed into the 2014 breakfast coffee program. And the fountain itself kept churning: Sierra Mist became Mist Twist in 2016, reverted in 2018, and was killed entirely in January 2023 when PepsiCo replaced it with Starry. Brisk Lemon Iced Tea, which had held the same valve since the late 1990s — a quiet 21-year run — was swapped for Brisk Raspberry in 2020, a decision that still generates active petitions.

What emerges from the full timeline is a pattern. Drinks at Taco Bell almost never die because customers stop ordering them. They die because something upstream changes: a corporate parent rebrands a product line, an operational format gets replaced by a faster one, a fountain valve gets reassigned to make room for an exclusive, or a PepsiCo brand decision ripples through every Yum! restaurant simultaneously. The drinks themselves are collateral. The decisions happen in boardrooms and supply-chain spreadsheets, and the first sign at the restaurant level is usually a new syrup box where the old one used to be.

01

Mountain Dew casualties

2012–2022
5 fountain flavors

Baja Blast proved that a single dedicated valve on a fast food fountain could become a cultural institution. What followed was a decade of PepsiCo trying to replicate that accident. Starting in December 2013, Taco Bell began cycling exclusive Mountain Dew flavors through a second dedicated valve — a spot on the fountain reserved specifically for Dew variants that existed nowhere else. Sangrita Blast held that valve for four years and built enough of a following that fans started petitions when it disappeared. Spiked Lemonade replaced it and lasted two. Kickstart Orange Citrus held on for seven years, quietly outlasting the others while never generating the same fervor. Each flavor lived and died on the same button. The valve still exists — Baja Midnight occupies it now — but the five flavors that cycled through it between 2012 and 2022 are gone. Some briefly appeared in retail cans. Most never left the fountain.

02

Frutista Freezes

2008–2013
10 flavors

Before the Freeze machine, before the frozen carbonated slush that now anchors the drink menu, Taco Bell tried something stranger: a blended fruit drink topped with actual strawberry slices, dispensed from its own dedicated equipment, using real fruit puree. The Frutista Freeze launched in May 2008 and ran for five years across three blender positions that rotated seasonally. At its peak, three different flavors ran simultaneously — a strawberry variant, a tropical variant, and a specialty blend, each cycling every few months. By 2010, the rotation was contracting. By 2012, most locations had stopped filling all three positions. The Freeze machine arrived in January 2013 and made the Frutista’s blender hardware obsolete overnight. The technology was fundamentally different — frozen carbonated granita versus blended fruit puree — and the Frutista’s operational complexity (real fruit, seasonal sourcing, three separate blenders) couldn’t compete with a machine that dispensed from a hopper. Ten flavors across nine documented seasons, and then the entire category was gone.

03

Freezes — LTO & collab

2013–present
40+ flavors

The Freeze machine that replaced the Frutista in 2013 is a multi-chamber granita unit: carbonated liquid kept frozen and churned until it reaches the consistency of a dense slush. Slot 1 has always been Baja Blast Freeze — permanent, untouchable, the anchor. Slot 2 was semi-permanent, typically holding a staple like Wild Cherry or Blue Raspberry. Slot 3 is the one that matters for this archive: a rotating LTO position that cycles every few months, hosting everything from candy brand collaborations to exclusive Mountain Dew variants to promotional tie-ins. Since 2013, more than thirty distinct flavors have occupied that third slot. Most last one season. A few, like Starburst Strawberry, came back multiple times. The Freeze LTO program is Taco Bell’s most active engine of beverage experimentation — and its most prolific source of discontinued drinks. Every flavor documented here once occupied that slot, served its window, and gave way to the next.

04

Limeade Sparklers

~2010–2012
2 drinks

The Limeade Sparklers were Taco Bell’s most direct attempt to compete with Sonic’s drink menu — hand-mixed, served over ice, built on Sierra Mist with real lime syrup and a lime wedge clipped to the cup. They launched around 2010 in two flavors: Classic Limeade and Cherry Limeade. The drinks required assembly behind the counter — soda from the fountain, syrup from a bottle, ice, a physical lime wedge — which made them operationally expensive for a chain built on speed. They lasted roughly two years. The Freeze machine rollout in early 2013 reorganized what the counter could support, and the Sparkler’s Sierra Mist base was already on its way out as a brand. On TikTok, videos mourning the Cherry Limeade Sparkler still surface regularly, years after the last one was served — more than almost any other item in this archive.

05

Hot beverages

2004–2020
5 drinks

Every item in this section died for the same reason: Taco Bell’s customer base does not visit for hot beverages, and every piece of equipment behind the counter competes for space with the Freeze machine and fountain drinks that actually drive traffic. Folgers held the coffee brewer for a decade — 2004 to 2014 — not because it was thriving, but because nobody had a reason to replace it. The 2014 national breakfast launch finally did: Taco Bell developed its own branded coffee program, which is still on the menu today, and retired the Folgers partnership as part of a broader push to make mornings matter. That same expansion added seasonal hot chocolate, a hot tea program at locations with the counter space for it, and a one-year Starbucks test partnership that ended without going national. By the late 2010s, Taco Bell tried one more angle with an iced coffee program at select markets — cold, caffeinated, aimed at a younger afternoon crowd — but it never scaled either. What survived all of this is a Rainforest Alliance–certified drip coffee and the Cinnabon Delights Coffee. Five other products documented here were each tried, each tested, and each removed when the math didn’t work.

06

Iced teas & lemonades

1978–2020
4 drinks

The non-carbonated valves on a Pepsi-house fountain are afterthoughts by design. Every major innovation at Taco Bell — Baja Blast, the Freeze machine, the dedicated Dew valve — is carbonated. The iced tea and lemonade slots exist because the fountain has room for them and PepsiCo has brands to fill them with, not because anyone walks into Taco Bell for a glass of Brisk. And yet Lipton Brisk Lemon held its valve for twenty-one years — 1999 to 2020 — longer than most food items last on the menu, longer than the entire Frutista program, longer than every Mountain Dew exclusive combined. It was replaced by Brisk Raspberry, a flavored variant swapped in without announcement. The lemonade slot, by contrast, has been volatile: Minute Maid (a Coca-Cola brand at a Pepsi house, if the attribution is correct), Dole Strawberry Lemonade, Snapple Lemonade for roughly one year, and then whatever came next. These are the drinks that fill the margins of the fountain — never the reason anyone visits, but always there until they’re not.

07

Defunct fountain sodas

2003–2025
4 brands

Most drinks in this archive were discontinued because Taco Bell chose to replace them — a new flavor took the valve, a new machine made the old format obsolete, a test program didn’t scale. The drinks in this section are different. They disappeared because PepsiCo killed the brand. Sierra Mist held the lemon-lime valve at Taco Bell for twenty years, then PepsiCo discontinued the entire product line in January 2023 and replaced it with Starry — a reformulated soda with a different sweetener and a younger-skewing brand identity. Sierra Mist’s market share had dropped to 0.1% nationally; the vast majority of its remaining volume came from captive Pepsi-house fountains at chains like Taco Bell and KFC. Pepsi Max lived on TB’s fountain as a zero-calorie option before being pulled around 2016 (the brand continued at retail as Pepsi Zero Sugar). Tropicana Twister occupied the juice slot until the non-carbonated section was simplified. These are collateral casualties — drinks that didn’t fail at Taco Bell, they failed everywhere.
