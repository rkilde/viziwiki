Overview

The first iPod touch that felt finished.

The original iPod touch launched in 2007 as a screen-first proposition — a pocket-sized multi-touch display for music, the web, and eventually apps. It was remarkable hardware for its moment, but it shipped with one obvious gap: no speaker. Listening required headphones, always. The second generation closed that gap, and in doing so made the device feel categorically more useful.

Announced on September 9, 2008 at Apple's "Let's Rock" music-focused media event, the second-generation touch debuted alongside updated iPod nano and iPod classic models. It shipped the same day at $229 (8 GB), $299 (16 GB), and $399 (32 GB). The price positioned it squarely as a premium music device — not a phone, but closer to the total iPhone experience than anything else Apple made.

The redesign was substantial. The flat aluminium back of the first generation gave way to a polished stainless steel curved back that matched the iPhone 3G's aesthetic. The device became 30% thinner — 8.5 mm at its thickest, tapering toward the edges — and lighter at 115 grams. External volume buttons appeared on the left edge for the first time, the headphone jack was recessed flush with the casing, and the internal speaker sat behind a small grille at the top of the back panel.

Nike+ wireless support arrived without any external adapter. Where the original required a small receiver plugged into the dock connector to communicate with Nike+ sensor-equipped shoes, the second generation handled it natively over a built-in wireless module. For runners already in the Nike+ ecosystem, this alone was a meaningful upgrade.

Internally, the device carried a Samsung-manufactured ARM processor running at 533 MHz — faster than its predecessor — paired with 128 MB of RAM. It launched running iPhone OS 2.1 with full App Store access, a category the original touch received only via a $9.95 paid software update in 2008. The second generation had it from day one.

Software support extended through iOS 4.2.1, released in November 2010 — a notably extended lifespan for a device discontinued in June of that year. After discontinuation, the third generation (September 2009) continued with the same external design, differing primarily in processor speed. The second generation's form factor was, in effect, the template that defined the iPod touch's look for four years.

Generation delta

What changed.
1st → 2nd generation.

The iPod touch 2nd generation was not a reinvention of the first — it was a completion of it. Every significant gap in the original was addressed: the missing speaker, the missing volume buttons, the recessed headphone jack, the external Nike+ receiver, the flat back that didn’t sit well in the hand. On the software side, the 2G shipped into a live App Store with 60 million downloads already recorded, with access included free on day one — no paid upgrade required.

Configurations

Four SKUs. One finish.
Only storage differs.

The iPod touch 2nd generation was sold in a single finish — polished stainless steel with a black front bezel — across four distinct SKUs. At launch in September 2008, three storage tiers were available: 8 GB, 16 GB, and 32 GB, priced at $229, $299, and $399 respectively. Every unit shared the same processor, RAM, display, speaker, Nike+ wireless, and battery.

All three launch models were discontinued simultaneously in September 2009, replaced by a single revised 8 GB "MC" model at a new entry price of $199. The revision patched a hardware-level jailbreak exploit and shipped with iPhone OS 3.1 pre-installed.

Cultural impact

The gateway to
mobile gaming.

The iPod touch 2nd generation launched in September 2008 — two months after the App Store opened with 500 applications. Games were the dominant category from the start, and the 2G was the first iPod touch to ship with native App Store access. Where the 1st generation had been a music player that could browse the web, the 2nd generation arrived as a pocket gaming console that happened to play music.

The timing was structurally significant. The 2G’s 3.5-inch multi-touch display, built-in accelerometer, and responsive capacitive screen made it a natural fit for the wave of touch-native games that defined the App Store’s first year: Tap Tap Revenge, Fieldrunners, Rolando, Flight Control. These were not ports of console games — they were designed from scratch for a touchscreen, and the iPod touch was the cheapest way to play them.

Apple recognized this quickly. By 2009, the company’s marketing had shifted to position the iPod touch explicitly as a gaming device. The “Funnest iPod ever” campaign ran television spots showing games, not music. Steve Jobs showed game demos at keynote events. In multiple quarters between 2008 and 2010, iPod touch unit sales exceeded those of the Nintendo DS and Sony PSP.

The absence of a cellular contract was the key. The iPhone cost $199 plus a two-year AT&T commitment at $60–100 per month. The iPod touch 2G cost $229 with no recurring cost. It introduced a generation of users to the concept that a pocket-sized touchscreen could be a primary gaming device — a market category that did not meaningfully exist before the App Store.

Jailbreaking

Breaking the lock.
What the underground built.

The iPod touch 2nd generation arrived at a moment when Apple’s control over its platform was absolute and the limits of that control were acutely felt. The App Store was new, approval was opaque, and an entire category of software the device was technically capable of running — multitasking, system-level customisation, third-party app stores — was simply unavailable. Jailbreaking changed that. For a significant portion of 2G owners, a jailbroken device was not a modified device; it was a complete device.

The BootROM window

The original “MB” series models contained a hardware-level vulnerability in the device’s BootROM — read-only firmware burned into the chip at manufacture and impossible to patch via software update. In 2009, George Hotz (GeoHot) released purplera1n, exploiting this vulnerability for a tethered jailbreak. Because the vulnerability was in hardware, it was permanent: no future iOS update could close it. Every original 2G unit could be jailbroken for the rest of its operational life.

Apple’s response was the “MC” model in September 2009 — an externally identical device with a patched BootROM (version 359.3.2) that closed the hardware vulnerability. The original MB units, however, remained permanently open, creating a secondary market where older 2G hardware commanded a premium among users who specifically wanted the unexploitable variant.

The Cydia ecosystem

The practical entry point for most jailbroken users was Cydia, a package manager and alternative app marketplace created by Jay Freeman (saurik). Where the App Store required Apple’s approval, Cydia did not. It distributed system-level tweaks, apps that duplicated functionality Apple reserved for itself, and developer utilities requiring root access. At its peak in 2009–2010, Cydia processed over 4 million unique devices per day with a catalogue exceeding 50,000 packages.

The cultural dimension was real. Jailbreaking was, for a generation of young users, the first encounter with the concept that software could be modified, that platform restrictions were choices rather than facts, and that a community of developers working outside official channels could produce software that matched or exceeded what the platform holder offered. The 2G’s permanent exploit made it the device of record for this community.

Market positioning

The budget
smartphone alternative.

In 2008, the iPhone was not a mass-market device. It required an AT&T contract — the only carrier that sold it — at a minimum monthly cost of roughly $70. Over two years, the total cost of ownership exceeded $1,900. For a significant segment of the market, iOS was appealing but the economics were prohibitive.

The iPod touch 2nd generation addressed this directly. At $229 for the entry model, with no contract, no monthly fee, and no carrier lock-in, the 2G delivered the core of the iPhone experience over WiFi: the same App Store, the same Safari, the same Maps, Mail, YouTube, Calendar, and Music. The only things missing were cellular data, a phone, and GPS.

Apple announced 20 million cumulative iPod touch sales by February 2009, less than five months after the 2G launched. The device found its largest audiences among students, teenagers, international users in countries where the iPhone had not yet launched, and cost-conscious consumers who wanted iOS without committing to a carrier.

The revised 8GB model at $199 in September 2009 pushed this further. The iPod touch became the volume driver that expanded the iOS installed base beyond what the iPhone alone could have achieved — validating Apple’s belief that there was a market for a non-phone iOS device at a consumer price point.

Industrial design

Design blueprint
longevity.

The iPod touch 1st generation had a flat aluminium back, sharp edges, and a recessed headphone jack. The 2nd generation replaced all of it: a curved, polished stainless steel back that tapered toward the edges, matching the iPhone 3G's silhouette. Volume buttons appeared on the left edge. The speaker sat behind a grille at the top. The headphone jack sat flush.

This design became the template. The 3rd generation, announced in September 2009, was externally identical: same curved back, same dimensions, same button placement, same speaker position. The only way to distinguish a 2G from a 3G was to check the model number.

The 4th generation in September 2010 added a Retina display, cameras, and the A4 chip but retained the fundamental design language: curved stainless back, chrome edge, front-face proportions. The 2G's form factor defined what the iPod touch looked like for four consecutive hardware generations spanning 2008 through 2012.

It was not until the 5th generation in October 2012 that Apple broke the pattern entirely, introducing a thinner aluminium unibody with a 4-inch display. The stainless-and-chrome design language that the 2G had introduced was retired after four years.

Operating system support

Nine versions. Two eras.
One hardware ceiling.

The iPod touch 2nd generation shipped with iPhone OS 2.1.1 in September 2008 and received its final update, iOS 4.2.1, in November 2010 — a support window of just over two years across ten distinct OS versions.

When iOS 4.0 arrived in June 2010, it was the first major upgrade delivered free to iPod touch users. But the 2G received the new interface without three of iOS 4’s headline features: multitasking, custom home screen wallpapers, and Bluetooth keyboard support. The hardware’s memory and processor architecture simply could not sustain them.

When iOS 4.3 shipped in March 2011, the iPod touch 2nd generation was absent from the compatibility list entirely — not partially supported, simply gone. The App Store ecosystem began to move away within months.

Controversies

Known issues and
public disputes.

Paid software upgrades

iPod touch users were charged $9.95 for the iPhone OS 2.0 update (App Store access) and $9.95 again for iPhone OS 3.0, while iPhone users received both for free. Apple attributed the fee to Sarbanes-Oxley accounting regulations. The explanation was legally defensible but commercially unpopular — particularly given that the 3.0 update unlocked Bluetooth hardware that had been physically present in their device since purchase.

Dormant Bluetooth chip

The 2nd generation shipped with a Samsung/Broadcom Bluetooth chip inside every unit from September 2008 onward, but the chip was disabled at the firmware level. It was not enabled until iPhone OS 3.0 in June 2009 — nine months after launch. Apple never publicly explained why the chip shipped dormant. The decision to charge $9.95 to unlock hardware the user already owned compounded the controversy.

iOS 4 feature exclusions

When iOS 4.0 was released in June 2010, the 2G received the update but was excluded from multitasking, custom home screen wallpapers, and Bluetooth keyboard support. Some users and developers disputed this, noting that jailbroken 2G units could run basic multitasking with acceptable performance, and that the wallpaper restriction in particular seemed more like a product decision than a hardware limitation.

Jailbreak and BootROM exploit

The original "MB" series models contained a hardware-level BootROM vulnerability that allowed unconditional jailbreaking — a persistent exploit that could not be patched via software update. Apple's response was the revised "MC" model in September 2009 with a patched BootROM. The original MB units remained permanently exploitable, creating a secondary market where un-patched 2G units commanded a premium.

Performance degradation on iOS 4

Users who upgraded to iOS 4.0 widely reported slower performance and reduced responsiveness compared to iPhone OS 3.1.3. Apple did not offer a way to downgrade once the update was installed, leaving users on a version of the operating system their hardware was not fully equipped to run smoothly. This pattern would recur across Apple's product line in later years and eventually result in regulatory scrutiny.
